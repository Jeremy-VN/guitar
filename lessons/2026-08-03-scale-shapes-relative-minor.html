1. The theory this lesson used

The major scale — the shape behind the shape

  • A major scale is a fixed pattern of steps: W–W–H–W–W–W–H (W = whole tone / 2 frets, H = semitone / 1 fret).
  • That half-step at the top is the "leading tone" — the note a single fret below the root, pulling back home. In E major that's D#→E.
  • Because the pattern is fixed, one fretboard shape can be slid to any key — learn the shape once, move it to wherever the root is.

Movable "shapes" (CAGED / the Leavitt five)

  • The neck can be covered with a handful of movable major-scale shapes (five, in the CAGED / Berklee "Leavitt" system).
  • There's no universal naming — one book's "shape 3" is another's "shape 2". Pick a reference (e.g. the Leavitt order) and stick to it. The shape in this lesson is one of those five.

Relative major & minor

  • Every major key shares all its notes with a minor key a minor-3rd below — its relative minor, which is the vi of the major.
  • So E major and C# minor are the same seven notes; the only difference is which note you treat as home. Same scale shape — resolve to E and it sounds major, resolve to C# and it sounds minor.

Where this goes next — modes (a quick mention — we didn't dwell on it)

Worth knowing in one line: keep the same box but make a different note "home" and you get the seven modes of the parent scale — your relative minor (Aeolian) is just one of them. Re‑centring gives modes; sliding the shape only changes key.

3. Part 2 — "Free Fallin'", refined

Start open — the "cowboy chords" (no capo, key of D)

Timing (4/4): hold D 2 beats, G 2, then a quick G (½ beat) into D 1 and Asus4 2 — then repeat.

  • The D drone lives in all three. The D on the B string (3rd fret) is common to D, G and Asus4 — plant your 3rd finger there and just move the others. D → Asus4 is barely any movement.
  • All downstrokes, gentle and even — pick barely protruding, pure tone, no plastic click. Count "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &" and resist speeding up after each change.
  • Let the top strings ring into one another.

4. Capo masterclass

  • Capos make a guitar go sharp. Pros either retune after a capo song or get handed a second guitar already set up with the capo — rather than fighting one guitar all night.
  • Match the capo radius to your fretboard. Common radii: 7.25" (vintage Fender), 9.5" (modern Fender), 12" (Gibson — your Les Paul). A capo that's too curved for the board clamps the high E and pulls it sharp.
  • Your current capo looks too curved for the 12" Gibson — you want a flatter one. Shubb make them in different radii; a shop here may not stock them all, so check the model for your guitar.
  • Adjustable (wheel) vs fixed. An adjustable one lets you set tension just tight enough to ring without buzzing — no tighter, or it goes sharp. A fixed capo clamps the same hard everywhere.
  • The higher up the neck you clamp, the sharper it tends to go (fatter neck, same pressure). Ideally don't go past the 7th fret; retune when you do.

5. Suggested practice order

  1. The E major scale shape, slowly, ascending and descending — even eighth notes with a metronome.
  2. Feel the two homes: resolve phrases to E (major), then to C# (relative minor).
  3. Slide the shape down 2 frets and play it in D.
  4. Guitar 1 riff (capo 3): D–Asus4–G, 3rd finger glued to the D drone, all downstrokes, dead-even tempo.
  5. Guitar 2 (capo 1): the E-shape changes on the beat, add the light 16th tickles once it's steady.
  6. Set the capo properly first (radius, tension, tune after fitting).
