The Modes — the complete picture

What each mode is, how it feels, where it's used, and how to play them from one shape

A mode is what you get when you take one parent scale and treat a different note as home. The seven notes of the major scale give seven modes — same notes, seven different "gravity centres", each with its own colour. Playing the C major scale but resolving to D gives D Dorian; resolving to G gives G Mixolydian, and so on.

Two ways to think about them, both useful:

  • Derivative (re‑centring): one scale shape, seven homes. Great for seeing that they're all the same notes. (This is the trick in the 3 Aug lesson — your relative minor is just the Aeolian mode.)
  • Parallel (same root): keep the root fixed and alter one or two notes of the major/minor you already know. Best for actually hearing each mode's flavour against one tonic.

Brightest to darkest

Order the modes from most sharpened to most flattened and each step simply lowers one more note by a semitone — a clean "bright → dark" dimmer switch:

brightest Lydian → Ionian → Mixolydian → Dorian → Aeolian → Phrygian → Locrian darkest (♯4) (major) (♭7) (♭7,♭3) (natural (♭2 …) (♭2,♭5 …) minor)

Lydian is major with one note raised (the brightest); each move right flattens one more scale degree until Locrian, which has the most flats and an unstable ♭5.

Each mode in detail

Ionian — the major scale

1 2 3 4 5 6 7. The plain major scale: fully at rest, bright, wholesome.

Feel: happy, stable, "home". Genres: pop, folk, country, classical, worship — most Western melody. Commonly cited: "Let It Be", the bulk of the pop charts. Tip: your default over any major key.

Dorian — the cool minor

1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7. A minor scale, but the natural 6th lifts the sadness — minor with hope and groove.

Feel: soulful, funky, hopeful minor. Genres: jazz, funk, soul, rock, Celtic/folk. Commonly cited: "So What" (Miles Davis), "Oye Como Va" (Santana), "Scarborough Fair", "Get Lucky" (Daft Punk). Tip: over an Em groove, add the natural 6 (C#) → E Dorian.

Phrygian — the dark one

1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7. Minor with a ♭2 right above the root — instantly Spanish / flamenco, or heavy and menacing.

Feel: dark, tense, exotic. Genres: flamenco, metal, film tension, Middle-Eastern-tinged music. Commonly cited: flamenco generally; palm-muted metal riffing (much of Metallica's rhythm work); Joe Satriani "War". Tip: lean on the ♭2 a fret above the root — that's the whole flavour.

Lydian — the dreamer

1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7. Major with a raised 4th that floats and never quite settles — wondrous, cinematic.

Feel: dreamy, magical, hopeful-but-unresolved. Genres: film scores, prog, jazz fusion, dream-pop. Commonly cited: The Simpsons theme (Danny Elfman), "Flying in a Blue Dream" (Joe Satriani), Steve Vai. Tip: hang on the ♯4 over a major chord for the "movie" sound.

Mixolydian — the rocker

1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7. Major with a flat 7th — the dominant / blues-rock sound: happy but earthy, made to groove.

Feel: bluesy, driving, good-time. Genres: blues, rock, funk, jam bands, Celtic. Commonly cited: "Sweet Home Alabama", "Norwegian Wood", much of the Grateful Dead. Tip: it's the sound of a I–♭VII–IV vamp (e.g. D–C–G).

Aeolian — the natural minor

1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7. The natural minor scale — this is the relative minor. The ♭6 is what makes it darker than Dorian.

Feel: sad, serious, introspective. Genres: rock, pop ballads, metal, classical minor. Commonly cited: vast — natural-minor rock/pop broadly (e.g. the "Stairway to Heaven" intro region). Tip: your default over any minor key.

Locrian — the outsider

1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7. The ♭5 makes even the home chord diminished, so it never rests — rarely a whole song, more a colour.

Feel: unstable, dissonant, unresolved. Genres: fringe metal, and jazz over half-diminished (m7♭5) chords. Commonly cited: Björk "Army of Me"; brief passages in metal. Tip: use it over an m7♭5 chord, not as a key to write in.

Playing them on guitar

One shape, seven homes (derivative): take a major-scale shape you know — like the box in the 3 Aug lesson — and resolve your phrases to a different note of it. Same fingering, new mode.

Same root, alter a note (parallel): keep the root fixed and nudge one degree of the major or minor you already play —

  • Major → Lydian: raise the 4th.   Major → Mixolydian: flatten the 7th.
  • Minor (Aeolian) → Dorian: raise the 6th.   Minor → Phrygian: flatten the 2nd.

The parallel way is best for hearing the difference, because the root stays put and only the colour note moves.

A caveat worth keeping: labelling a whole song with a single mode is usually a simplification, and guitarists argue about it endlessly. Treat the example tracks as commonly cited illustrations of a sound, not strict facts — the useful thing is the character each mode carries, which you can hear for yourself.
