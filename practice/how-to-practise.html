How to Practise This: what the evidence actually says

A review of the learning-science literature, applied to fretboard note-name training · July 2026

Short answer to your question: don't spend a week on A. Mix 3–5 confusable notes per session, twice a day, 10 minutes each, and come back to every note every 2–3 days.

The reasoning is below, but the strongest argument isn't the one most people cite. It isn't "interleaving is better" — that literature is weaker than its reputation. It's that a week of nothing but A removes the retrieval step entirely, and retrieval is the single best-evidenced ingredient in the whole field.

1. The one-paragraph honest summary

  • There is no direct research on learning the guitar fretboard. None. Not one controlled experiment on fretboard note-name learning, tab vs notation, or note-naming speed on a stringed instrument. Everything below is transfer from adjacent literatures, and I've flagged where that transfer is a stretch.
  • Two findings are strong enough to build on: retrieval practice and spaced practice. Both have meta-analyses in the tens of thousands of participants, and both replicate in real classrooms, not just labs.
  • Most of the rest — interleaving, contextual interference, sleep consolidation, the "85% rule", desirable difficulties as a general principle — is weaker than the popular accounts suggest, and several parts of it have failed to replicate outright.
  • Expect lab effect sizes to shrink substantially in real use. A 2024 meta-analysis of spaced retrieval across nine university STEM courses found +2.1% [0.2, 4.0], significant in only 2 of 9 courses.[1] The lab says d ≈ 1; the field says a couple of percent. Reality is somewhere between, and closer to the field than anyone selling a method will admit.

2. Your actual question: one note for a week, or mix?

Mix. But the reason matters, because it changes how you mix.

Why a week of A is the wrong shape

  1. It abolishes retrieval. If every answer for a week is "A", you are no longer performing position→note retrieval. You are performing position recognition with an invariant response. Carter & Grahn put it well: repeating an event does not mean repeating the processing of that event — re-solving 143 + 247 immediately just retrieves "390" without doing any addition.[2] Retrieval practice beats re-study at g = 0.50 across 61 studies, rising to 0.59 for paired associates and 0.61 for cued recall specifically.[3] Single-note blocking removes exactly the operation you're trying to train. strong
  2. Your real difficulty is discrimination, not identification. The failure mode isn't "I can't find an A". It's "is that an A or a G♯?" That's a between-category discrimination problem with high between-category similarity — adjacent frets are one semitone apart and look nearly identical. Similarity structure is the master moderator of interleaving benefits: the more confusable the categories, the bigger the gain from juxtaposing them.[4] The best applied demonstration is a cluster-randomised trial of interleaved maths practice in 787 US middle-school students: 61% vs 38% at a one-month unannounced test, d = 0.83.[5] Same structure as your problem — those students could execute every procedure but couldn't tell which one applied. strong
  3. A week on one note is massed practice. Seven consecutive days on one item set and then never returning is close to the worst schedule available. Spacing is the most robust finding in applied memory research.[6] strong
  4. You'd be practising in a context that never occurs. In actual playing, notes arrive unpredictably. moderate

The honest counter-argument (don't over-correct)

  • Interleaving backfires for verbal/word material: g = −0.39 in the definitive meta-analysis (59 papers, 238 effect sizes, N = 8,466). Blocking won.[4] Note names are partly verbal material, so this is a genuine warning against maximal mixing.
  • Interleaving reliably hurts novices on complex tasks. In tennis, Frisbee and other real-world skills, low-skilled learners did better with blocked practice.[7] Meta-analytically, contextual-interference benefits in young/novice learners are ~zero (d = 0.02–0.12, n.s.).[8]
  • The contextual-interference literature itself is methodologically poor: mean statistical power 0.43;[9] 0 of 34 studies rated methodologically strong in a 2024 review;[8] and applied-setting effects collapse to non-significance on removal of two outliers. Two 2024 meta-analyses directly contradict each other and each criticises the other's methods. This is not settled science.

The reconciliation: block at the scale of minutes, interleave at the scale of the session. Introduce a new note on its own for 2–5 minutes, then fold it into a mix. The unit of blocking is "the first few exposures", not "a week".

Four small studies, two supporting, one null, one clear reversal. Anyone telling you interleaving is established for musical practice is overreaching. What is consistent across these studies is the metacognitive illusion — see §6.

3. The protocol

Daily

  • Two sessions of 10–15 minutes, at least 3–4 hours apart. This is the highest-leverage single change available. In the study closest to your task — 51 arbitrary paired associates, 20 repetitions total — splitting those same 20 repetitions across 5 sessions instead of 1 gave 78% vs 15% retention at 36 hours (d = 1.72). Identical total work.[10] strong
  • Make one of them the last thing before bed. Not because sleep is magic, but because it minimises the waking interference that follows.[11] moderate
  • 3–5 note names per session, chosen to be confusable — chromatic neighbours (F/F♯/G), or the same name across several strings. Not 1, not all 12. judgement, evidence-guided
  • Every note revisited every 2–3 days, not once per week-long block. strong on principle

Within a session

  • Retrieve, then check — always. Say the note before the app confirms it. Cued recall g = 0.61 vs recognition 0.29.[3] strong
  • Immediate feedback on every miss. With feedback g = 0.73; without, 0.39 — and if your unaided accuracy drops below ~50%, the benefit falls to g = 0.03, i.e. nothing.[3] strong
  • Target 80–90% correct. Hard enough to be effortful, easy enough to succeed. Among successful retrievals, harder is better — increasing the number of intervening items before a repeat took final recall from .30 to .54.[12] moderate
  • Stop at about 3 correct recalls per item. Overlearning on day one is subadditive with later spaced sessions: the "3 + 3" rule (3 correct recalls, then 3 spaced relearning sessions) gave durable retention at 1–4 months across N = 533.[13] The 5th within-session repetition is far worse value than the 1st repetition in a new session.[14] moderate
  • Keep the items contiguous. Don't check your phone between questions. Inserting 10–30 s of unrelated activity dropped the interleaving benefit from g = 0.73 to 0.22 (n.s.); inserting trivia questions abolished it entirely.[4][15] moderate

Across weeks

The optimal gap between sessions scales with how long you want to retain it — roughly 10–20% of the target retention interval, though observed optima range 6–37%.[16] The curve is asymmetric: too short is much worse than too long.[17] When in doubt, wait longer.

~8 sessions over ~6 months per item set. Bahrick's 9-year vocabulary study got 76% recall at 5 years from 26 sessions at 56-day spacing.[18]

Two things the trainer can't do for you

  • Practise both directions separately. Testing benefits do not transfer to rearranged stimulus–response pairs.[19] "Position → note" and "note → all positions" are two item sets, not one. That's exactly why the trainer has both modes — use both.
  • Do it on the guitar too. An 8-hour adaptive speeded music-reading drill produced a 44% improvement in reading threshold (η2 = 0.44) — and zero transfer to anything else.[20] Screen drilling makes you fast on a screen. Name the note as you fret it, name the target before you land on it, name what you just played.

4. The single highest-leverage move isn't scheduling at all

Learn the structure, not 72 facts. The fretboard is highly regular: 12-fret octave repeat; identical intervals between all adjacent string pairs except G→B; strings 1 and 6 are the same notes; octave shapes at +2 strings/+2 frets and +3 strings/−3 frets. Interleaving benefits shrink when an explicit rule is available and taught directly[21] — because you've reduced the number of independent items to memorise.

Practical order: the natural notes on low E and A first (they anchor everything via octave shapes and the CAGED system), then derive the rest, then drill the residue. Which is also why starting on Basic with strings 5 & 6 in the trainer is the right opening move, not an arbitrary one.

Corroborating this: the only controlled study of how guitarists actually encode the fretboard (n = 30 experienced players, mean 21 years) found their knowledge is viewpoint-dependent visual-spatial and motor, not verbal. Chord charts and photographs drove the hand directly; chord names incurred an extra processing cost.[22] Note names are a layer you add on top of an intact shape/interval representation — not a replacement for it, and not something you route your playing through.

5. What to ignore

  • refutedExpanding intervals are better than fixed intervals. They aren't. g = 0.034 across 54 effect sizes.[23] The decisive experiment held total spacing constant and varied only the schedule shape: absolute spacing η2 = .24, relative spacing η2 ≈ 0.[24] What matters is that the gaps get long, not that they get longer. Still taught as fact almost everywhere.
  • refutedThe "85% rule" for optimal difficulty. Derived analytically for gradient-descent binary classifiers; never tested on human declarative memory; the number is noise-model dependent (75–85%).[25] The memory data, if anything, point higher. Good slogan, bad citation.
  • refutedSleep produces a ~20% overnight jump in motor skill. The classic finding[26] does not survive de-confounding for data averaging, reactive inhibition and time-of-day. Bias-corrected, the post-sleep gain is negative and indistinguishable from wake.[27][28] Sleep stabilisation remains plausible; sleep enhancement of motor skill does not. (Declarative memory is a different story — see §3.)
  • refutedMicro-breaks add learning. The performance jump after short rests is real and replicated (N ≈ 951), but when both groups are tested under matched conditions the advantage vanishes within seconds, and it occurs even for non-repeating sequences.[29][30] It's reactive-inhibition dissipation plus motor pre-planning. Take breaks to keep your repetitions accurate — that's worth plenty — but not for free consolidation.
  • refutedMusic training makes you smarter. With active controls and proper randomisation, g ≈ 0.[31]
  • refutedMaking material harder to read (odd fonts, blurring) aids learning. Recall d = −0.01, transfer d = 0.03, across 3,135 participants. It reliably made things feel harder and take longer, and did nothing.[32]
  • no evidenceAny guitar app improving guitar skill. Zero peer-reviewed trials for Yousician, Rocksmith, or any fretboard trainer — including this one. The mechanisms they use (forced retrieval, immediate feedback, spacing, adaptive difficulty) are individually well-evidenced; the products are not.

6. The finding that will actually bite you

Your subjective sense of which practice method is working will be wrong, and it will stay wrong even after you see evidence to the contrary.
  • In the landmark study, 78% of participants learned better under interleaving, and 78% judged massing more effective — and they made that judgement after taking the test that showed the opposite. In the second experiment, 64 of 72 got it backwards.[33]
  • Pianists' judgements of learning favoured blocked practice (ηp2 = .22) while random practice produced better 2-day retention.[34] Clarinettists: 6 of 10 judged interleaved practice more useful, and 6 of 10 still preferred blocked.[2]
  • Postal workers learning typing under distributed practice learned far better and were less satisfied with their training.[35]
  • The mechanism is processing fluency: the second consecutive exposure to an item feels easy, and ease is misread as learning.

What to do about it: ignore how the session felt. The trainer logs accuracy and average response time per day precisely so you have a delayed, objective read instead. Judge the method by next week's numbers, not today's fluency. And expect a drop from day 1 to day 2 on the better schedule — that's the normal shape, not failure.

7. What nobody knows

  • How long fretboard note-name automaticity actually takes. The nearest number: 8 hours of adaptive speeded drilling produced a 44% threshold improvement in music reading.[20] So: probably 8–20 focused hours, not 100 and not 1. That is an extrapolation, not a measurement.
  • Whether note-name fluency transfers to sight-reading or improvisation. Untested, and the transfer literature predicts it won't without specific practice of those tasks.
  • CAGED vs intervals vs octave shapes vs string-by-string; tab vs notation. All untested. Not "weakly supported" — untested.
  • What an adult beginner can ultimately achieve on a stringed instrument. Nobody has measured it. Relevant, though: once practice volume and genetics are controlled, early starting age has no demonstrated causal effect on musical achievement (monozygotic co-twin analysis, 299 discordant pairs, β = 0.00, p = .93).[36] The "you missed the window" narrative is not supported.

8. One clinical note

The only solid practice-volume risk signal in the literature: pianists who developed focal dystonia had comparable childhood practice to controls but escalated daily practice volume more steeply in their 20s and 30s, and high daily volume significantly increased risk on logistic regression.[37] String players carry roughly 2.9× the wrist/hand pain odds of wind players, and no preventive intervention has demonstrated efficacy in a controlled trial.[38] Ramp gradually. You'll recognise the phenomenology of task-specific, painless, instrument-only loss of fine motor control better than most — it warrants stopping, not practising through.
